> **Disclaimer:** Claim denials, denial codes, and payer-specific criteria vary significantly by insurance plan. Always refer to your provider agreement, payer website, or provider relations team for current information about denials and appeals.


Claims for [psychological testing](/services/psychological-evaluations/), [therapy](/services/therapy/), and [medication management](/services/medication-management/) are all subject to denial. Testing claims are denied at higher rates than simple outpatient services for reasons specific to how they are coded and authorized. Therapy and medication management denials often stem from documentation or medical necessity issues. Understanding the denial landscape helps providers and patients respond effectively regardless of service type.

This is part 7 of our series on billing for mental health services in Kentucky.

## Why Mental Health Claims Are Denied at Higher Rates

Before diving into specific denial codes, it helps to understand the structural features of mental health billing — especially psychological testing — that increase denial risk.

- **Timed codes require precise documentation.** [96136, 96137, 96130, and 96131](/blog/billing/common-cpt-codes-used-in-psychological-testing) are all time-based. The number of units billed must match the documented time. Payers that request records will count the hours in the note. Similarly, [therapy codes](/blog/billing/cpt-codes-therapy-services) such as 90837 require session-time documentation.
- **Authorization requirements create failure points.** If testing proceeded without required PA (or the wrong codes were authorized), the entire evaluation claim may be denied. Therapy and [medication management](/blog/billing/cpt-codes-medication-management) visits can be similarly denied when required PA was not obtained.
- **Diagnosis-code mismatches.** The ICD-10 codes in Box 21 must support the medical necessity of the specific service delivered. Testing for ADHD billed with a diagnosis that does not reflect attention or executive function complaints will raise questions. The same applies to therapy and prescribing visits.
- **Multiple dates, multiple claims.** Each service date for a testing evaluation has its own claim submission requirements, and errors on one claim can affect others in a linked evaluation sequence.
- **UHC same-day billing.** Submitting UHC testing codes piecemeal rather than together results in systematic denial.

## Common Denial Reasons for Mental Health Claims

### 1. Missing or Invalid Prior Authorization

**Denial code:** Often CO-15 (authorization number not on file) or CO-4 (service not covered without authorization)

This is the most financially significant denial for psychological testing. It also applies to any service — therapy modality, psychiatric evaluation, or medication management visit — where the specific plan required PA. If the payer required prior authorization and it was not obtained, the services are typically non-covered and the provider cannot bill the patient.

**Common Pitfall Workarounds:**
- Confirm whether authorization was required by reviewing the payer's current PA requirements
- If authorization was required and not obtained, a retroactive authorization request may be possible for some payers (this is not guaranteed)
- If authorization was requested but not yet approved when services were rendered, document the pending request
- Appeal on the basis of the clinical circumstances and any evidence that authorization was requested before services were provided

### 2. Not Medically Necessary

**Denial code:** Often CO-50 or CO-57 (not medically necessary based on plan criteria)

The payer's clinical reviewer determined that the evaluation did not meet medical necessity criteria — either because the documentation was insufficient or because the testing wasn't considered necessary for the presenting diagnosis.

**Response:**
- Request the specific medical necessity criteria the payer used to deny the claim
- Compare those criteria to the clinical documentation from the 90791 intake
- Prepare an appeal letter that specifically addresses each criterion the payer used
- Include supporting documentation: the intake note, any prior records, referral documentation, and a clinical rationale narrative written by the treating psychologist

The appeal letter should directly quote the plan's criteria and explain, with clinical evidence, why the evaluation meets those criteria. A generic appeal without clinical specificity rarely succeeds for medical necessity denials.

### 3. Authorization Number Missing or Incorrect

**Denial code:** Often CO-4 or CO-15

The authorization was obtained but not entered correctly on the claim (Box 23 of the CMS-1500), or the authorization number was transposed.

**Response:**
- Look up the original authorization confirmation
- Resubmit the claim with the correct authorization number in Box 23
- This is a corrected claim, not an appeal — most payers will accept a corrected claim submission rather than requiring a formal appeal for administrative errors

### 4. Diagnosis Code Does Not Support Service

**Denial code:** Often CO-57 or a payer-specific "diagnosis insufficient for services billed" code

The ICD-10 diagnosis codes in Box 21 do not justify the CPT codes billed. For example, billing 96130 (psychological testing evaluation services) with only a mild adjustment disorder code may trigger a medical necessity denial because the payer doesn't consider that diagnosis to warrant comprehensive neuropsychological testing.

**Response:**
- Review the clinical record to confirm whether additional or more specific diagnosis codes were present and not included
- If the documentation supports a more specific or additional diagnosis, a corrected claim with updated diagnosis codes may resolve the denial
- If the documentation doesn't support more specific codes, an appeal with a clinical narrative explaining the medical necessity within the documented diagnoses is needed

### 5. Incorrect or Outdated CPT Codes

Psychological testing codes have changed over time. Older codes (such as 96101, 96102, 96103, 96111) were replaced by the current code set in 2019. Using outdated codes results in an invalid code denial.

**Response:**
- Resubmit with the correct current CPT codes (96130, 96131, 96136, 96137)
- Confirm that your billing system's code library is current

### 6. Taxonomy Code Mismatch

If the taxonomy code on the claim does not match the taxonomy enrolled with the payer for that NPI, the claim is typically denied as the provider is "not found" in the payer's system.

**Response:**
- Verify the taxonomy code in NPPES for the rendering provider's NPI-1
- Confirm the taxonomy code in your EHR provider profile
- Confirm the taxonomy enrolled with the payer during credentialing
- Correct the claim and resubmit; if the payer's records are incorrect, call provider relations to update the enrollment record

### 7. Timely Filing Exceeded

**Denial code:** CO-29 (time limit for filing has expired)

The claim was not submitted within the payer's timely filing window.

**Response:**
- Timely filing denials are the hardest to appeal. Most payers will only accept an appeal if the provider can demonstrate that:
  - The claim was submitted on time but rejected due to a clearinghouse or payer system error (attach the original submission receipt)
  - The delay was caused by retroactive eligibility changes or enrollment issues outside the provider's control
- Document any system rejections or enrollment delays that explain why the claim was held

### 8. UHC Codes Submitted Separately (Not on Same Claim)

For UHC, submitting psychological testing codes on multiple separate claims instead of together results in denials on the later claims as duplicates or as not matching the authorization requirements.

**Response:**
- Void the separately submitted claims
- Resubmit all codes on a single claim covering all service dates
- Confirm the authorization covers all service dates before resubmission

### 9. Duplicate Claim

Submitting the same claim more than once — or submitting dates and codes that overlap with a previously submitted claim — results in a duplicate denial.

**Response:**
- Confirm the original claim was received and adjudicated (check the ERA or payer portal)
- If the original was paid, no action needed
- If the original was denied, correct the denial and resubmit (not as a second new claim, but as a corrected or re-billed claim)
